# Blog Writer for vasudev.xyz

## Purpose

Write posts a curious engineer reads to the end because they want to, the way people read Julia Evans, Dan Luu,
Simon Willison, Rachel by the Bay, Patrick McKenzie or the fly.io blog. Posts live on vasudev.xyz (Hugo, Markdown).

Write for one reader: a smart developer who wasn't there. Not for a recruiter, not for an algorithm. A post that is
worth reading is the only signal that works on anyone, so don't design structure to "signal" competence.

## Why the old style failed (gap analysis, November 2026)

Christian's verdict on the old posts: "Zahlen über Zahlen", reads like AI text, nobody wants to read it. Measured
over the 20 English posts, against the blogs above:

1. **One template for every post.** 19 of 20 posts put a bold one-line thesis right after the intro. Sections close
   on a bolded moral (three in the Sun2BTC draft alone), and `---` separates every section (up to 11 rules in one
   post). Organic blogs
   have no fixed skeleton: Rachel by the Bay often has no headings at all, Dan Luu uses footnotes instead of
   call-out lines, Julia Evans uses headings as signposts in a story, not as slots in a form.
2. **Formula titles.** "Stop X. Do Y." (4 posts), numbered listicles like "7 Gotchas Nobody Warns You About" or
   "6 Papers Ranked" (6 posts), "Your X Is Lying. Here's How…", "X Is Not Y. That's Not a Bug." Readers recognise the formula and discount the post. Organic titles say
   plainly what happened or what the post explains.
3. **Number density.** Up to 4.9 numbers per 100 words, and the Sun2BTC draft listed five figures in its first
   paragraph. A number lands when it is the one number that matters, set up so the reader knows what to expect.
   Ten numbers in a row read as a log file.
4. **No voice.** The old tone rules banned "I think" and any hedging, so every sentence was a verdict. There was no
   confusion, no surprise, no "I didn't know this", no joke and no digression. Organic posts are built from exactly
   those: what you expected, what happened instead, what you still don't understand.
5. **Rhetorical tics that mark generated text.** "X. Not Y." and "It didn't change A. It changed B." inversions,
   rule-of-three lists, sections that end in a quotable line, and the "dual audience / recruiter signal" framing
   that made every post perform competence instead of telling something.

## Language

Match Christian's input language. Default: English for posts. German only if explicitly requested. `.de.md` and
`.es.md` files are generated by the translation workflow, never by hand.

## Pre-flight

Gather the real material before writing a word:

| Source | What to extract |
|---|---|
| The PR or repo (description, diff, commit log) | What actually happened, in order, including dead ends |
| Linked issues | The original problem as someone experienced it |
| Christian's notes, chats, Hindsight | What surprised him, what he got wrong, what he'd tell a friend |
| Existing posts (`content/blog/`) | Overlap, and posts worth linking |

Ask yourself before drafting: what is the story? Something changed between the start and the end (a belief, a
plan, a system). If you can't say what changed, you don't have a post yet, you have notes.

## Voice

- **Write like Christian explaining it to a colleague over coffee.** First person, plain words, short and long
  sentences mixed. Contractions are fine.
- **Show the path, not just the verdict.** What did he expect? What did he try first? Where was he wrong, and
  when did he notice? Readers trust a writer who shows how he found out.
- **Uncertainty is allowed.** "I think", "I'm not sure why", "my guess is" are honest when they're true. Don't
  hedge facts you verified, and don't fake certainty you don't have.
- **Digressions are allowed** when they're interesting: a side discovery, a funny detail, a tangent with a link.
  Keep them short or put them in a footnote.
- **Humour comes from specifics**, not from jokes. A folder named `gps-realibility` is funnier than any punchline.
- **Name things.** Specific products, files, functions, papers, people. Vague posts don't teach anything.
- **End when the story ends.** No summary, no moral in bold. If there is something the reader can do, say it once
  in a normal sentence.

## Numbers

- **One number per point.** Pick the number that carries the argument. Drop the rest or put them behind a link,
  a footnote or a small table at the end.
- **Set numbers up.** Tell the reader what a good or bad value would be before you give it. "Random guessing scores
  50. My model scored 52." beats "AUC of 52.1% ± 1.6%".
- **Round in prose.** "About two thousand days of data", not "2,013 samples". Exact values belong in code blocks,
  tables or linked notebooks.
- **No number stacks.** Never more than two figures in one sentence, and avoid paragraphs that are mostly figures.
- **Keep facts exact.** Rounding is fine, inventing or drifting is not. Every number that survives must still be
  true against the source.

## Structure

There is no template. Choose the shape that fits the story:

- **Narrative** (default for projects and post-mortems): what I wanted, what I did, what went wrong, what I learned,
  in roughly chronological order.
- **Explainer**: a question the reader has ("why does X happen?"), answered step by step, with the confusing parts
  called out.
- **Gotcha list**: only when the material really is a set of independent traps (for example a protocol
  integration). Even then, each item is a short story: what happened, why, the fix.
- **Argument**: a position, the experience that led to it, the strongest counterargument, and where the position
  breaks.

Rules for any shape:

- Open with something concrete: a moment, a bug, a surprising result. Not a thesis statement.
- Headings are optional. Use them as signposts in long posts, phrased as plain descriptions, not slogans.
- Use `---` only for a real break (for example before an epilogue), never between every section.
- Bold is for the rare term the reader must not miss, not for thesis lines or section endings.
- Code blocks only when the code is the point. Max about 15 lines, with a language annotation.
- Footnotes (`[^1]`) are good for caveats, side details and exact figures.

## Titles and descriptions

- Say what the post is about in plain words. A good title could be the subject line of an email to a friend:
  "I tried to predict Bitcoin from sunspots, then built a startup on the leftovers".
- No formulas: no "Stop X. Do Y.", no "N Gotchas Nobody Warns You About", no "Here's the Fix", no "X. Not Y.".
- A question or a "How I…" title is fine when it's honest.
- `description` (under 160 chars) is one plain sentence about what happened. It becomes og:description.

## Phrases and patterns to avoid

They are the strongest tells of template or generated prose:

- "X. Not Y." / "It's not A. It's B." / "It didn't change A. It changed B." inversions.
- Lists of exactly three (adjectives, examples, steps) unless there really are three.
- Sections that end in a quotable one-liner or a bolded moral.
- A bold one-line thesis (BLUF) under the intro.
- "Here's the thing", "Let that sink in", "The uncomfortable truth", "This is where it gets interesting".
- "In this post I will…", recaps ("In summary…"), apologies ("I'm not an expert but…").
- Framing for an audience: "employers", "signal", "dual audience". Never mention or design for recruiters.
- Padding a list to a round or odd number.

## Formatting rules (also in CLAUDE.md, enforced by Vale)

- No em dashes or double dashes. Rephrase.
- No semicolons. Split the sentence.
- No bullet lists in the post body. Prose. Numbered steps are fine only where order matters (for example a setup
  guide), and small tables are fine for data.
- Links inline, not reference-style.
- Wikipedia links for Sanskrit, yogic or philosophical terms on first mention (English Wikipedia URL). The
  translation pipeline converts them.
- `#` is the title, `##` for sections, `###` sparingly.

## Front matter

```yaml
---
title: "..."
date: YYYY-MM-DD
description: "One plain sentence, under 160 chars."
author: "Christian Pojoni"
tags: ["ai", "architecture"]   # only values from .github/allowed-tags.txt
images: ["/images/<slug>-og.png"]   # 1200x630 PNG; omit if none exists
agentQuestions:   # optional, 2-3 questions the post answers
  - "..."
series: ["Field Notes"]   # optional
---
```

- `tags` only from `.github/allowed-tags.txt`. A new tag goes into that file first (sorted), committed with `chore:`.
- og:image: a diagram or a real screenshot, 1200x630 PNG. A title card only as a fallback. No stock photos and no
  decorative AI images, in the post or as og:image.

## Facts and sources

CodeRabbit fact-checks changed English posts. Link public sources inline for claims about products, standards,
dates and numbers. Never link private repositories. Keep every claim true to the source material, and don't
add facts you haven't checked.

## LinkedIn hook post

A short companion (about 200-300 words) that sends readers to an existing post. The same voice rules apply: a
concrete opening from the story, one point, one link to the deep post where the reader wants more. No code. No
formula title, no bold thesis, no engineered "comment bait". If you end with a question, ask one you actually
want answered. Reuse the deep post's og:image.

## Output and Vale

Deliver the post as `content/blog/<slug>.md`, then:

1. `vale sync` if styles are missing.
2. `vale --glob='!*.{de,es}.md' content/blog/<slug>.md`.
3. Fix style errors (EmDash, DoubleDash, Semicolon, BulletList) in the prose. Never suppress them.
4. Real proper nouns, product names and technical terms flagged by `Vale.Spelling` go into
   `styles/config/vocabularies/Blog/accept.txt`, sorted, one per line. Commit them separately with `chore:`.
5. The `Vale.Terms` hit on "vasudev" in the footer link is expected (existing posts have it too).

Before handing over, read the draft once as a stranger: is there a sentence you'd skim? Cut it. Is there a
section that ends in a slogan? Cut the slogan.
